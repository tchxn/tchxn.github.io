## The work

Brand guidelines for Melbourne Vicentre Swimming Club: the primary mark and a full lockup family, an alternate **MVC** mark, a palette that fixes a red-on-navy contrast problem, a type system, and a digital asset kit — logo files, colour codes, typefaces, templates. Then the part that gets seen from the stands: the club's apparel.

## The system

- **Navy leads, red is an accent only.** Navy carries most of any branded item. Red is kept for outlines and the bar — it links to the national team cap stripe — and is never a background.
- **Red washes out on navy at a distance.** Early feedback showed the red disappearing from poolside and on camera, so anything that has to read at range gets a white keyline or a white bar.
- **Two type voices.** A collegiate display face for the marks, Franklin Gothic for the bar and lockups, and a plain sans for everything else. Nothing small on a uniform — if it can't be read from the other side of the pool, it isn't there.
- **Don't look like the neighbours.** Colour combinations that could be confused with other Victorian clubs are ruled out in the guidelines.

## Apparel

The vertical mark was the board's favourite, but it only worked on the back of a garment. The fix was to stop treating a garment as one canvas: **front and back are two canvases, each gets the orientation that fits it.** The hoodie carries the landscape MVC on the front and the vertical mark on the back; the T-shirt reverses it.

After seeing the mockups, the board dropped the "Since 1988" line — it's still visible in these drafts.

## The commercial side

This is the project where I learned the business half of design.

- Scoped and quoted the work as a retainer rather than hourly, because the hours aren't the product
- The board **countered** with a different structure. I accepted it, with a turnaround caveat that protected me against the risk their version introduced
- **Retained master and working files**; final deliverables are supplied as PDF, PNG and JPEG, with a buyout priced separately
- Terms agreed in writing before any work began

Design is the easy part. Being trusted with a budget and a board is a different skill, and it's the one that turns a freelancer into a supplier.
