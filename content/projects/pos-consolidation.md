## The situation

A family-owned food factory and distribution business in Papua New Guinea runs two sites, each a retail front with a warehouse behind it. They ran on four separate point-of-sale systems that didn't talk to each other.

Each system lived on one computer at one site, with one person entering stock into it. When stock moved from a warehouse to a store, nothing tied the two records together, so stock went missing with no way to trace where. When the directors travelled, they couldn't see their own business: they had to ask staff to check the system and report back. A stocktake meant counting by hand, then someone in accounts checking each item against the system, one line at a time.

## What I did

My part was the requirements, not the decisions. I traced the stock loss to the transfers between warehouse and store, where no shared record existed. When the vendor proposed an upgrade, I went through it against what the business actually needed and separated required modules from optional ones before anyone signed. Then I carried the directors' requirements into the scoping meetings with the vendor and brought back what was possible. I also sourced compatible hardware independently, which saved money.

The directors made the calls. The vendor built and configured the system.

## What changed

Both sites and the warehouse now run on one cloud system as three channels, with one view of every item and how it moves.

- **The directors can run the business from anywhere.** Sales, stock and supplier setup are visible and editable from any location with an internet connection, including from overseas.
- **Duplicated work moved to head office.** Each site no longer needs its own person entering the same data. A full-time accounts staff member at the second site was freed from duplicated work for other duties.
- **Head office can check any site by logging in,** instead of sending someone there.
- **Stocktakes are scanned and verified against the system in one step,** instead of a manual count reconciled line by line.

In the directors' words it is not perfect, but it is much better than before.

## The decision I'd point to

The two sites didn't get the same design, and that was the right call. At the site that takes overseas container orders, stock arrives in bulk, often 300 to 2,000 units of one item, while the shelf shows 10 or 12. There, the warehouse is now a separate stock location, so every transfer to the store is recorded. At the smaller site, stock turns over within about a week in small quantities, and logging every transfer would cost more work than it saves, so warehouse and shelf stay as one pool. That was the directors' decision. It's the part of the project I learned most from: the right control depends on the volume moving through it, not on applying one rule everywhere.

The handheld scanners can also add, remove and adjust stock. On the floor they are used only for price checks and stocktakes, and stock adjustments stay with head office as a control.

## Where it stands

Live. The business runs on it. One site still runs warehouse and shelf stock as a single pool, by design.
